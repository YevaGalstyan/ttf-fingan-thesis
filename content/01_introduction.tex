\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
\renewcommand{\thefootnote}{\ifcase\value{footnote}\or*\or**\or***\fi}

Changes in the gas price affect macroeconomic conditions and the costs of individual companies and households. Since transmission capacity cannot be expanded in the short term, supply responds slowly, and imbalances are resolved primarily through price changes. Consequently, the gas price is sensitive to weather conditions, industrial activity and the prices of coal and emission allowances. The strongest price movements follow supply disruptions. In 2025, the import dependency of the European Union for natural gas reached \SI{87.6}{\percent} \parencite{eurostatGasSupply2026}. This dependency exposes the European market to external supply shocks. The most severe of these shocks occurred in 2021 and 2022, when demand recovered after the pandemic and Russian pipeline flows declined. The disruption of 2026 is the most recent example.\footnote{Both disruptions are discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:gas-markets}.} Such shocks create price risk for all participants in the gas market. In this context, the question of how to model gas price movements has become increasingly relevant.

This chapter introduces the problem of generating realistic natural gas price paths for option pricing. The difficulties of modeling gas price movements are explained first. Generative adversarial networks (GANs) are then introduced as a data-driven approach to this problem. On this basis, the research question is formulated, followed by the contribution of the thesis. Finally, the structure of the remaining chapters is outlined.

\subsection{Problem Statement and Motivation}\label{sec:motivation}
In Europe, natural gas is traded at virtual hubs, of which the Dutch Title Transfer Facility (TTF) is the most liquid \parencite{heatherEuropeanTradedGas2026}. Futures and options on TTF are traded on ICE Endex. Physical and financial market participants can use these contracts to limit their exposure to price volatility \parencite[241]{gemanCommoditiesCommodityDerivatives2006}. To quote these contracts, value their positions and manage risk, market participants can rely on pricing models. The price of an option is its expected payoff in a risk-neutral world, discounted at the risk-free rate \parencite[292]{hullOptionsFuturesOther2022}. For this reason, pricing models rest on an assumption about the distribution of the futures price at expiry. A widely used model for pricing options on futures is Black-76 \parencite{blackPricingCommodityContracts1976}. It assumes that the futures price follows a geometric Brownian motion (GBM), under which log returns are normally distributed with constant volatility. 

The prices of natural gas futures do not match this assumption. They exhibit seasonality, revert to a long-term level and show sudden price spikes \parencite[244]{gemanCommoditiesCommodityDerivatives2006}. Hence, the implied volatility\footnote{Implied volatility and the volatility smile
are defined in Sec.~\ref{sec:implied-vol}.} of options on natural gas is not constant across strike prices, as it would be under the assumptions of Black-76. This pattern, known as the volatility smile, is skewed to the right, since the market is more sensitive to price rises \parencite[101--105]{gemanCommoditiesCommodityDerivatives2006}. 

Parametric stochastic models add features of observed prices to the GBM. The Merton jump-diffusion model extends the GBM with a jump process, which allows for sudden large price changes \parencite{mertonOptionPricingWhen1976}. Commodity models add mean reversion \parencite{schwartzStochasticBehaviorCommodity1997} and the convenience yield, the value of holding the physical commodity, which moves around a long-term level \parencite{gibsonStochasticConvenienceYield1990}. The GARCH model targets the constant volatility assumption by letting the volatility depend on the size of the past returns \parencite{bollerslevGeneralizedAutoregressiveConditional1986}. Fitting a GARCH model requires choosing the model order and the distribution of the random shocks in advance. The accuracy of these models depends on whether the assumed form matches the actual behavior of the underlying asset. 

In contrast, machine learning models do not require specifying the functional form of the price process in advance. A large literature applies neural networks to option pricing, using characteristics such as moneyness and time to expiry to reproduce observed prices \parencite{rufNeuralNetworksOption2019}. These models do not simulate the path of the underlying futures price. Generative models address this limitation. GANs, introduced for image generation,\footnote{The GAN architecture is explained further in Sec.~\ref{sec:gans}.} learn the distribution of the data by training two competing networks, a generator that produces samples and a discriminator that judges them \parencite{goodfellowGenerativeAdversarialNetworks2014}. Conditional GANs (cGANs) extend GANs by providing a condition for controlling the generated samples \parencite{mirzaConditionalGenerativeAdversarial2014}. ForGAN is a cGAN for time series forecasting, where the condition is the sequence of past observations \parencite{koochaliProbabilisticForecastingSensory2019}. Fin-GAN extends ForGAN by rewarding correct directional forecasts during training and was evaluated on equity returns \parencite{vuleticFinGANForecastingClassifying2024}. Returns generated by such a model can be compounded into price paths, and the option price is estimated as the average discounted payoff over these paths.

GAN architectures for financial time series \parencite{yoonTimeseriesGenerativeAdversarial,wieseQuantGANsDeep2020,niConditionalSigWassersteinGANs2020,zhangVisualRealismReliable2026} and GAN-based simulators of option markets \parencite{wieseDeepHedgingLearning2019,vuleticVolGANGenerativeModel2024} have been developed and evaluated on equity data. In energy markets, GANs have been applied to scenario simulation \parencite{walterProbabilisticSimulationElectricity2024,kornSimulatingIntradayElectricity2025}. For natural gas, GANs have been used to estimate crisis-risk indicators \parencite{bohorquezcorreaTimevaryingSystemicRisk2026}. These studies do not generate price paths and evaluate them through option prices. Results on equity data cannot be assumed to transfer to natural gas. Whether GAN-generated returns reproduce the properties of TTF futures returns, and whether the derived option prices match the market, remains open.

\subsection{Research Question}\label{sec:research-question}

This thesis examines whether GANs can generate TTF futures returns with the stylized facts of the observed data. These include heavy tails, asymmetry and volatility clustering. In addition, it assesses whether price paths generated by recursive rollout reproduce the market prices of TTF options.  A positive answer would provide a model that conditions on the market state without assuming a parametric form for the return distribution.

\subsection{Own Contribution}\label{sec:contribution}
This thesis applies ForGAN and Fin-GAN to daily TTF front-month futures returns. The generated returns are compared with the test data across training lengths, hidden sizes of the generator and discriminator, condition window lengths and input scaling methods. Each configuration is trained with five random seeds and reported with mean and seed spread. The configuration with the smallest seed spread is selected for further training with the cost function terms of Fin-GAN. The selected model is used to generate price paths by recursive rollout. The paths are adjusted to the risk-neutral world with a correction adapted from \textcite{jin-chuanduan1995}. From these paths, call options are priced by Monte Carlo simulation on \num{20} valuation dates, each about one month before a monthly expiry. The resulting prices are converted into implied volatilities and compared with the market implied volatilities across moneyness ranges. GBM and two bootstrap modes serve as benchmarks.

\subsection{Structure}\label{sec:structure}
