 \subsection{Implied Volatility}\label{sec:implied-vol}
As described in Sec.~\ref{sec:options}, the price of an option depends on the distribution of $F_T$ in the risk-neutral world. \textcite{blackPricingCommodityContracts1976} gives a closed-form solution for Eq.~\eqref{eq:price-call} and Eq.~\eqref{eq:price-put} under the assumption that $F_T$ is lognormal. The result is known as the Black-76 model. The model further assumes that $\sigma$ and $r$ are constant over the life of
the option and that the option is European. 

A random variable $X$ is normally distributed with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$ if its probability density is
\begin{equation}
f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}}
       \exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
\end{equation}

The distribution is symmetric around $\mu$ and is fully described by $\mu$ and $\sigma$. For the standard normal distribution $\mu = 0$ and $\sigma = 1$. A random variable $X$ is lognormally distributed if $\ln X$ is normally distributed. 

Under the lognormal assumption, the European call and put prices are given by
\begin{equation}
c = e^{-rT} \left[ F_0 N(d_1) - K N(d_2) \right],
\label{eq:black-76-call}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
p = e^{-rT} \left[ K N(-d_2) - F_0 N(-d_1) \right],
\label{eq:black-76-put}
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution, $F_0$ is the current futures price, $K$ is the strike price, $T$ is the time to maturity, $r$ is the risk-free rate, and $\sigma$ is the volatility of the futures price, as defined in Sec.~\ref{sec:financial-markets}. The Eqs.~\eqref{eq:black-76-call} and~\eqref{eq:black-76-put} do not contain a growth term for $F_0$, since the futures price is not expected to grow in a risk-neutral world (Sec.~\ref{sec:options}). $d_1$ and $d_2$ are given by

\begin{equation}
d_1 = \frac{\ln(F_0/K) + \sigma^2 T/2}{\sigma \sqrt{T}}, \qquad
d_2 = d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{T}.
\end{equation}

The parameters entering Eq.~\eqref{eq:black-76-call} and Eq.~\eqref{eq:black-76-put} are observable, except for $\sigma$. The implied volatility is the value of $\sigma$ that makes the model price
equal the market price of the option. Eq.~\eqref{eq:black-76-call} and Eq.~\eqref{eq:black-76-put} cannot be solved for $\sigma$ in closed form, since $\sigma$ enters through $N$ and appears in both $d_1$ and $d_2$. As a result, the implied volatility is found numerically. The option price increases with $\sigma$, so exactly one value reproduces the observed price \parencite[358]{hullOptionsFuturesOther2022}.

If the Black-76 model held, the implied volatility of options on the same futures contract with the same expiry would be constant across strikes. The plot of implied volatility against $K$ is known as the volatility smile. In practice the smile is not flat. The distribution of $F_T$ implied by the market prices of options, referred to as the implied distribution, therefore departs from the lognormal \parencite[101--102]{gemanCommoditiesCommodityDerivatives2006}.

In equity markets, the implied volatility decreases as the strike price increases, since equity prices and volatility are negatively correlated \parencite[456]{hullOptionsFuturesOther2022}.
In energy markets the smile is skewed to the right, since participants pay more for protection against a sharp rise in prices. This was found for crude oil, heating oil, gasoline and natural gas
\parencite[105]{gemanCommoditiesCommodityDerivatives2006}.
